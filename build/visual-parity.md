## What the first tool lets you DO that the fourth does not

**1. Narrow by clicking the chart itself — three ways.**
- Image 1: "Click a zone on the aircraft to keep only what was found there" — click the fuselage drawing or a legend row to filter the whole corpus to that zone. Fourth: "Point at a zone to read what was found there" — hover only, and the zone boxes all show 0, so there is nothing to click through to.
- Image 2: "Drag across the months to take a period" — brush an arbitrary span out of 380 months. Fourth: no month chart is reachable in the shown view; no drag, no brush.
- Image 3: "Click what the crew had to do" — click "Unscheduled landing" to cut 1,757,828 down to 112,189. Fourth: the FORCED tab exists but the shown rail offers no clickable action rows.

This is the biggest single loss: the first tool is a filter you operate; the fourth is a page you read.

**2. Combine filters.** In the first, WHEN, WHERE, WHO and FORCED all stay live in the grey strips (image 2, mid-page) — you can carry a zone into a time drag into a crew action. The fourth answers one question about one thing at a time; there is no visible way to ask "unscheduled landings on this tail, 2023 onward."

**3. Scope the time window.** Image 1–3: "AIM AT a month or year" + "Take it", or "OR ONE DAY" date picker. Fourth: the subject is fixed to its whole file history ("Jul 1997 to Mar 2008"); no way to bound dates.

**4. Open a single report.** Images 1–3: "One report from this selection… Click to open the full report →" with the FAA's own filing and the mechanic's verbatim words. Fourth: "Show the verified summary" and "Export CSV" — aggregates only. A journalist who needs to quote one write-up, with its date and ATA code, must export and dig manually.

**5. Follow paths out.** First tool footer: Search, Patterns, Aircraft, How it was found, Fleet, Story leads, New defects, Same day many aircraft, Same defect, Corrosion & cracks, Old airframes, Engines, Compare, Every code explained, Method — each carrying your selection. Fourth: one link ("lookup desk"). No next step after reading.

**6. Browse the WHO rankings.** First tool carries tail and airline lists (the "900 most-reported aircraft" strip). Fourth only accepts a typed query; you cannot discover a tail, only confirm one.

## Where the fourth breaks the first tool's discipline

- **It draws numbers it then disowns.** "Where on the aircraft the 69 findings sit" above nine zone boxes every one reading 0, with the footnote "the zone count is from the sample read, not the whole file." The first tool's rule is stricter: it draws only the 212,940 reports it can place and says plainly, in prose, that 1,454,504 "cannot be drawn." The fourth charts a sample and lets the zeros imply an answer the caption contradicts.
- **A definition filed as a limitation.** Under "WHAT THIS PAGE CANNOT SHOW," the third bullet — "A write-up is a defect that was found and recorded, usually during maintenance" — is a glossary entry, not a blind spot. The first tool keeps definitions in footnotes and reserves the confessions for real gaps (late-arriving reports, unzonable text).
- **Heading promises five, tabs show four.** "One thing, five answers" lists "what was found" among the five, but only WHEN / WHERE / WHO / FORCED appear as tabs. The first tool never advertises a rail it doesn't render.
- **"Point at a zone to read what was found there"** is an instruction the page cannot honour — there is nothing found there to read.

## Ranked by what a journalist loses most

1. Click-to-narrow (zone, month-drag, crew action) — without it, no cross-cutting question can be asked at all.
2. Per-report click-through with the FAA filing and verbatim text — without it, nothing is quotable or citable at first hand.
3. Time bounding — without it, "is this getting worse?" is unanswerable.
4. The outgoing links that carry a selection — without them, one finding leads nowhere.
5. WHO ranking browsing — you can verify a tail but never discover one.
