# The four that matter most, and why

**Old airframes.** "It is the proportion of reports in each age band that record
corrosion at all, which compares like with like. The raw counts do not, because
the bands hold very different numbers of aircraft." Without this the panel is a
bar chart saying middle-aged aircraft break most, which is only true because
there are more of them.

**Engines.** "Only about three in a hundred reports name the engine, so this is a
count inside a small subset, not a failure rate per engine in service. Use it to
find cases to check, never to rank manufacturers."

**What the crew did.** "Not what broke, but what it forced." And: "A high share
on a few hundred reports is not the same finding as a high share on tens of
thousands, so read the Reports column beside it."

**Same day, many aircraft.** "When one airline writes up the same system on
several different aircraft on a single day, the cause is rarely the aircraft. It
is a batch of parts, a procedure, a supplier or a shared inspection." And the
counter-warning: "When the same airline clusters on the same system on many
separate days, you are usually looking at a scheduled inspection working through
the fleet. The 'other days like this' column is there to tell the two apart: a
low number is the interesting case."

That last pair is the shape to aim for throughout. Say what the panel shows, then
say the reading that would be wrong, then say what tells the two apart.

# Where it goes

Each panel gets a short block under its heading, before its figures: one or two
sentences on what it shows and why it is worth looking at, and one on how it can
be misread. Where a caveat belongs to a specific column or table, put it beside
that table rather than at the top.

Keep it plain. No headings inside it, no bullet lists, no bold. The original
writes in sentences and so should this.
